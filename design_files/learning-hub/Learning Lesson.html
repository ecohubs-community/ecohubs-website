The forms: village, cohousing, commune

Four recognisable forms, distinguished by exactly one question: what is held in common? Get this straight and half the confusion in the field disappears.

9 min read·Foundational·part of a 74-minute guide
The short version
  • Cohousing shares facilities and governance; homes and income stay private. Works best at 15–40 households.
  • Ecovillages share land and infrastructure and design their systems for regeneration. 30–300 residents.
  • Communes share income and property; the highest trust requirement and the smallest number of examples.
  • Co-ops and land trusts share ownership only — a legal layer that can sit under any of the other three.

One question sorts them

People argue about labels because they are arguing about different things: architecture, ideology, legal form, size. Set those aside and ask the only question that predicts daily life — what is held in common? Everything else follows from the answer.

Hold that question next to any project you visit and its category becomes obvious within an hour, regardless of what its website says.

Cohousing

Cohousing keeps private, self-contained homes and clusters them around shared facilities — nearly always a common house with a kitchen large enough to cook for everyone. Residents manage the whole thing themselves, which is where the governance load lives.

It is the least demanding form to join and the easiest to leave, which is precisely why it scales: a household can participate fully without restructuring its finances. The trade-off is that shared life has to be actively organised. Nothing forces you into the common house.

What to look at on a visit

Count how many people are in the common house on an ordinary Tuesday. Cohousing that works has a busy common house on days when nothing is scheduled.

Ecovillage

An ecovillage is defined by intent at the scale of a settlement: it designs its social, ecological, economic and cultural systems so the place ends up better than it was found. Housing tenure varies wildly — owned, leased, rented, or held by a trust.

Because the shared object is infrastructure, this is the form where permaculture, water systems and energy decisions become collective questions with real money attached. It is also the form most likely to run a visitor programme and a training centre, because education is one of the few reliable income streams at that scale.

Commune

A commune shares income and usually property. Membership means economic interdependence, not just proximity: earnings go into a common purse, and needs are met from it, often mediated by labour credits so that cooking and accounting are counted on the same scale.

This is the form with the strongest levelling effect and the highest trust requirement. It also demands the most transparent bookkeeping of any model — when there is one purse, every member is effectively an auditor.

Co-ops and land trusts

These are not lifestyles, they are containers. A community land trust holds land permanently and leases it to residents, taking the land itself out of the speculative market. A housing co-op holds buildings and grants members occupancy rights. Either can sit underneath cohousing, an ecovillage or a commune.

Choosing the container is a legal and financial decision with a twenty-year horizon, and it is the one most forming groups postpone longest. Do it before money changes hands, not after.

Which one fits you?

  1. Start from money, not values. Can you restructure your income? If not, cohousing or an ecovillage — and that is a perfectly good answer.
  2. Then ask about time. Shared infrastructure needs maintenance. Four to ten hours a week is normal; be honest about what you have.
  3. Then ask about visibility. The more you hold in common, the more of your life the group sees.
  4. Only then look at places. Most people do this in reverse, fall for a photograph, and discover the mismatch on day sixty.
Before the next lesson

Write one sentence: "I am willing to share ____, and not ____." Keep it. It will do more work than any comparison table when you are standing on someone's land, being charmed.
