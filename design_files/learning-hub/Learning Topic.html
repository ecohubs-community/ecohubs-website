Intentional Communities

Learn what intentional communities are, how they actually work day to day, and how to find or build one without repeating the mistakes that end most attempts.

18 min read·Foundational·Updated 2 August 2026·~40 min to complete with links
The short version
  • An intentional community is a group who chose to live together around shared values and explicit written agreements. The agreements are what separate it from a nice neighbourhood.
  • The main forms are cohousing (private homes, shared facilities), ecovillages (settlement-scale ecological design), communes (shared income), and co-ops and land trusts (shared ownership only).
  • Most communities fail on governance and money, not on idealism. Decide how you decide before you buy anything.
  • Joining costs somewhere between a rental deposit and a house deposit, depending on whether members own, lease or rent.
  • Visit before you commit. Every established community has a guest track; use it twice, in different seasons.

What is an intentional community?

An intentional community is a group of people who have chosen to live together, or close to one another, around shared values and a set of explicit agreements. The word doing the work is intentional: the arrangement is deliberate, and the terms of it are written down.

That last part is what separates a community from a friendly street. Neighbours share a place. A community shares a place and a decision-making process, a way of holding money, and an agreed answer to what happens when someone stops carrying their share.

Intentional community

A group of people who have chosen to live together, or in close proximity, around shared values and explicit agreements — including how decisions are made and what happens when they are broken.

Communities differ enormously in how much they share. At one end, a cohousing project shares a garden, a common house and a monthly meeting, while every household keeps its own income and front door. At the other, an income-sharing commune holds everything in a common purse and decides collectively what each person needs. Both are intentional communities. The distance between them is the interesting part.

A useful test

If the group disappeared tomorrow, what would you lose? A community answers with infrastructure — decisions, money, land, care. A social circle answers with company. Both are worth having; only one needs governance.

The forms they take

There is no official taxonomy, but four forms cover most of what exists. Groups routinely mix them: a legally constituted co-op running an ecovillage with a cohousing cluster inside it is entirely normal.

CohousingCommon house, outdoor space, some meals, governanceHomes, income, most possessions15–40 households

An ecovillage is defined less by its buildings than by its intent: it consciously designs its social, ecological, economic and cultural systems to regenerate the place it sits in. A community land trust, by contrast, is a legal instrument — it can hold the land under any of the other three forms.

Deep · where the labels mislead

"Ecovillage" is often used for projects with a permaculture garden and conventional governance, and withheld from dense urban co-ops with far lower per-capita footprints. Judge a project by its water, energy and decision systems, not by its category or its website.

How decisions get made

Ask any long-running community what nearly killed it and you will hear about meetings. Governance is the system by which a group makes, records, reviews and enforces decisions — including how it changes that system. Most groups adopt one of three families.

  1. Consensus — a proposal passes only if everyone agrees. Excellent for trust and shared ownership; brutal at scale, and vulnerable to whoever is most willing to keep talking.
  2. Consent — a proposal passes if no one has a reasoned, paramount objection. The bar moves from "is this the best idea?" to "is this safe enough to try?", which is why decisions actually close.
  3. Sociocracy — consent decisions inside nested circles, joined by double links, with roles elected by open nomination and fixed terms.

We didn't have a governance problem. We had a governance vacuum, and the loudest person filled it.

Founding member · a project that dissolved after six years

Whichever family you pick, the details that decide whether it survives are unglamorous: who may raise a blocking concern and on what grounds, what your quorum is, how long a role lasts before rotation, and where the written agreements live so that a member of two months can read them.

How long should a community meeting be?
Communities that report meeting satisfaction tend to run one 90-minute plenary a month with a published agenda and a hard stop, plus short circle meetings that handle domain decisions without plenary involvement. The failure pattern is the opposite: a three-hour weekly meeting where everything is decided by everyone, which burns out exactly the people who are most reliable.
Do we need a facilitator?
Yes, and preferably a rotating one. Facilitation is a role that serves the process rather than the outcome; when the person holding it also holds a strong position on the proposal, both jobs suffer. Small groups often trade facilitation between three or four trained members.

Deep · what the governance research supports

Elinor Ostrom's fieldwork on long-enduring commons found a consistent set of design principles: clearly defined boundaries and membership, rules matched to local conditions, participation by those affected in changing the rules, monitoring by the members themselves, graduated sanctions, accessible conflict resolution, and recognition of the group's right to organise. Every one of those maps directly onto a community agreement most projects skip.

Design principleWhat it looks like in a community
Defined boundariesA written membership process with a decision point, not open-ended guesthood
Graduated sanctionsA first conversation, then a circle, then a formal review — not silence, then expulsion
Accessible conflict resolutionA named repair process anyone can trigger without permission from leadership
Monitoring by membersTransparent budgets and work logs, read by the people they describe

Money, and who holds it

Community economics runs on a spectrum from fully separate to fully shared. Most projects sit in the middle: private income, a monthly contribution to a shared budget, and one or two collectively owned assets.

  • Separate income, shared costs. Members pay a monthly fee covering land, maintenance, insurance and reserves. Simplest to start, easiest to leave, weakest at redistributing.
  • Labour credits. Work for the community is counted in a shared unit so that cooking, accounting and roof repair are commensurable. Removes the quiet hierarchy of paid versus unpaid work.
  • Income sharing. All outside earnings enter a common purse; needs are met from it. Eliminates internal wealth gaps and demands a very high level of trust and transparency.
  • Sweat equity. Labour credited towards a member's financial stake — the usual way people without capital join a build-out.
Before the romance

Write the exit terms in the same meeting as the entry terms. What a leaving member is owed, over what period, and out of which account is the single most common cause of legal conflict in communities over ten years old.

What daily life is actually like

Most days are ordinary. People go to work — increasingly from a desk on site — cook, garden, argue about the compost, put children to bed. The differences are structural rather than dramatic: shared meals two or three times a week, a work quota measured in hours, a calendar with everyone's commitments on it, and a monthly meeting that is either the best or worst evening of the month.

The recurring surprise for newcomers is not the labour, it is the visibility. In a community your absence is noticed, your moods are read, and your decisions affect people who will still be there next year. Most people find this a relief. A minority find it unbearable, and that is legitimate — the trial period exists to find out which you are.

A typical week
  • 2–3 shared dinners
  • 4–10 hours of community work
  • 1 circle meeting for your domain
  • 1 plenary a month
What it is not
  • A retreat centre with residents
  • An escape from paid work
  • A place without conflict
  • Cheaper by default

What people gain

  • Fewer things, more capacity. One workshop, one set of tools, three cars instead of twenty. Shared infrastructure is the main reason community footprints measure far below national averages.
  • Care that does not depend on family. Illness, childcare, ageing and grief are held by more than one household.
  • Practical skill. People learn building, growing, facilitation and finance because the community needs them to, not as a hobby.
  • Belonging with terms. Membership you can read, question and change is a rarer thing than it sounds.

What breaks them

Honest accounting matters more here than encouragement. The recurring causes of collapse are consistent across decades and continents:

  1. No agreed way to decide before the first hard decision arrives.
  2. Founder dependency — one person holding the vision, the money and the keys.
  3. Money entering as friendship, with loans and gifts undocumented.
  4. Unspoken labour expectations, usually falling on the same three people.
  5. No repair process, so a single unaddressed conflict quietly becomes the culture.
The honest number

Most forming groups never reach land, and a large share of those that do dissolve within their first decade. The projects that last are rarely the most idealistic ones — they are the ones that wrote things down early and reviewed them often.

Frequently asked

How much does it cost to join?
Anywhere from a rental deposit to a house deposit. Rental-model communities ask for a deposit and monthly rent; equity models ask members to buy a share, commonly in the tens of thousands; land trusts lease at below-market rates with a small entry fee. Ask for the last three years of accounts before you commit anything.
Do I have to share my income?
Almost never. Fully income-sharing communities are a small minority. Most ask for a monthly contribution and a work quota, and are entirely uninterested in what you earn outside.
Can I bring children? Pets? A partner?
Children, usually yes and often gladly. Pets are a common source of written policy — ask early. Partners who have not been through the membership process are the more delicate case; most communities require both people to join as members.
What if I want to leave?
A well-run community has written exit terms: notice period, what you are repaid, over how long, and who buys your share. If a group cannot answer this in one paragraph, treat it as a warning.
Is this just for people who can afford it?
Not necessarily, but capital-heavy models do filter by wealth. Sweat equity, staged buy-in, community land trusts and rental membership all exist specifically to widen the door. Ask which of them a project uses — the answer tells you what it believes.
