<Quick>

Methods divide on one question, and it is not the one people argue about. **Three of them produce a picture** — a poll, a set of linked questions, a ranked election. Nothing passes and nothing fails; the group learns where it stands or which option wins. **Three of them carry a <Gloss term="motion">motion</Gloss>** — a written proposal is adopted or it is not: a threshold vote, <Gloss term="consensus">consensus</Gloss>, <Gloss term="consent">consent</Gloss>.

Choosing between them is a question about stakes, not about values. Consensus is slow and precise, which is right for the community's purpose and ruinous for the colour of the bathroom tiles. Almost every community that lasts ten years runs at least three methods, sorted by what the decision costs to get wrong.

</Quick>

Ask a community how it decides things and you will usually get one word back. Consensus, or consent, or "we vote". It is almost never true, and the gap is not dishonesty — it is that a group notices its formal method and stops noticing the four informal ones running alongside it.

The useful question is not which method is best. It is which decision gets which method, and whether anyone wrote that down.

## Producing a picture, or carrying a motion

Most confusion about decision methods comes from putting them all on one shelf. They do two different jobs.

Some methods **produce a picture**. They tell a group where it stands, or which of several options it prefers. Nothing is adopted at the end, because there was nothing on the table to adopt — no proposal, no yes, no no. These are for the stage before a decision, and using them well is the reason some communities' proposals pass easily: the disagreement was found and resolved before anything was drafted.

Others **carry a motion**. There is a written proposal, and at the end it is adopted or it is not. What separates them is what counts as a no — a shortfall against a threshold, an objection, or an objection that somebody has judged serious enough to stand.

A group that reaches for the wrong shelf produces a familiar mess: a poll on a question that needed a decision, so nothing is settled; or a binding vote on something the group had never discussed, so the losing half feels ambushed.

A **poll** asks members to pick one option and reports how the group split. Which weekend for the work party. Which of three names. It is the most under-used method in community practice, because groups reach straight for a binding vote on questions where nothing needed to bind.

A **multi-question poll** puts several linked questions out at once and tallies each on its own — _visitors should join work hours_, _there should be a maximum stay_, _hosts are responsible for their guests_ — before anybody drafts a visitor policy. What comes back is a map of where agreement already exists and where the real argument is. It is the single cheapest way to stop a proposal that was doomed from the first line. VoteCast calls this **Common Ground**; the practice has no settled name in the field.

A **<Gloss term="ranked-choice-voting">ranked vote</Gloss>** elects one option from several without splitting the vote. Five candidates for facilitator, and the winner is not someone twenty-two per cent wanted and everyone else could not stand.

### The three that carry a motion

A **threshold vote** puts a written motion to the group and adopts it if approvals clear an agreed share — more than half, or a <Gloss term="supermajority">supermajority</Gloss> for the weightier things. It is dismissed too quickly in community circles. It is fast, it is legible, and for reversible operational business it is usually correct. Its cost is structural: it manufactures a losing minority every time, and a minority that loses repeatedly stops attending.

<Callout type="note" title="A naming collision worth knowing">

VoteCast calls this method **Approval**, meaning approve-or-reject on a motion. In voting theory, _approval voting_ means something else entirely — voters approve as many candidates as they like, and the most-approved wins. Same word, different mechanism. Worth checking which one someone means before agreeing to it.

</Callout>

**<Gloss term="consensus">Consensus</Gloss>** asks whether anyone objects, and expects the proposal to change until nobody does. It produces buy-in nothing else matches, and the amendment loop is a genuinely good teacher — a group that has consensed on something carries it out because it owns it, not because it passed. Past a dozen people it begins handing a veto to whoever is most tired or most stubborn. A **<Gloss term="stand-aside">stand-aside</Gloss>** convention, letting someone disagree on the record without stopping the group, is what keeps it survivable.

**<Gloss term="consent">Consent</Gloss>** narrows the question again: not _do you object?_ but _do you have a <Gloss term="paramount-objection">paramount objection</Gloss>_ — a named harm to the group or its aim, judged serious enough to stand. The bar moves from enthusiasm to tolerability, and the burden moves onto the objector. Its standard is that a proposal be good enough for now and safe enough to try, which assumes a review date and quietly breaks without one.

The pair people most often confuse has a page of its own: [consensus vs consent](/learn/compare/consensus-vs-consent). So does the one this section is really about: [majority vote vs consensus](/learn/compare/majority-vote-vs-consensus).

## The settings that quietly decide fairness

A method name settles less than groups expect. Four numbers settle more, and each is a trade with a real cost on both sides.

**How many must take part.** The <Gloss term="quorum">quorum</Gloss>. Set it high and one quiet month blocks everything; set it low and five people decide for forty.

**What share passes it.** More than half, or two-thirds. A supermajority protects a minority from a thin majority, and hands a determined third of the group a standing veto over any change.

**When the tally becomes visible.** Live results let people see where the group is heading. They also let people follow it rather than think — the reason a running count and a deliberative decision sit badly together.

**What an absence means.** Whether silence counts as agreement or is simply left out. Silence-as-consent lets a busy group decide anything, including things most of them never read.

None of these appear in the name of the method, and a community that has answered them differently from yours is running a different system under the same word.

## No community needs only one

This is the part most groups arrive at the hard way, usually after an evening spent on tile colours.

Sort decisions by what they cost to get wrong. The RCOS Standard names three <Gloss term="decision-tiers">tiers</Gloss>, and they hold up:

- **Operational** — day-to-day business inside rules already agreed. The rota, the repair, the order. Fast, reversible, low ceremony. A threshold vote or a circle's own consent, decided in a day.
- **Strategic** — direction and real money. A new building, a loan, a change in what the land is for. Careful: a supermajority, a proper deliberation window, a delay before it takes effect.
- **Constitutional** — the community's purpose, its agreements, its membership rules, and how it decides. Slow on purpose: consensus, a long alignment phase, and a window in which a late objection still counts.

One rule makes the scheme work rather than leak: **a decision that cannot be clearly classified goes to the higher tier.** Without it, every borderline matter drifts downward, because the lower tier is always the easier meeting.

VoteCast ships this as presets — an operational preset at a two-thirds bar with a day of deliberation, a constitutional preset running consensus with a week of deliberation and a two-week objection window. The specific numbers are one community's answer, not the answer. The sorting is the transferable part.

<Callout type="caveat">

None of this touches whether the right people were in the room. A method decides how a decision is _closed_; it says nothing about whether the members most affected were asked, whether the proposal was written clearly enough to vote on honestly, or whether the group will live by a result it dislikes. Communities with an unresolved power problem produce bad decisions under every method on this page, and usually blame the method.

</Callout>

## What breaks

**The method is adopted in name only.** A group says "consent" without defining grounds for objection, so it is still unanimity. It says "consensus" and runs something closer to consent. Both are common, and both mean years of arguing about a rule nobody wrote down.

**One method for everything.** Whichever one it is, it fails at one end. Consensus everywhere exhausts the people with jobs and children until only the retired are left deciding. Majority everywhere carries constitutional change on a margin of one and creates a permanent minority in decisions about the community's own identity.

**The numbers were set after the vote.** A threshold or quorum decided once the result is visible is not a rule, and everyone present will remember that it was not.

<Deep title="Deep · why there is no single best rule">

The clearest theoretical account of why communities end up with tiers comes from economics rather than from the communities movement.

In _The Calculus of Consent_ (1962), James Buchanan and Gordon Tullock modelled the choice of decision rule as a trade between two costs that move in opposite directions as the required share rises.

**External costs** are what you bear when a decision goes against you — the rule you did not want, applied to you anyway. These fall as the threshold rises. Under unanimity they reach zero, because nothing can pass over your objection.

**Decision-making costs** are what it takes to reach agreement at all: the meetings, the redrafting, the evenings. These rise as the threshold rises, and near unanimity they rise very steeply, because the last holdout is the most expensive one to satisfy.

The optimal rule is where the two summed costs are lowest — and because that depends on how much the decision would cost you if it went the wrong way, **it is different for different classes of decision**. A high threshold is worth its cost for a change to the community's purpose. It is not worth its cost for a rota. That is the whole argument for sorting decisions into tiers, arrived at forty years before sociocratic practice reached the same place from a different direction.

Buchanan received the Nobel Memorial Prize in Economics in 1986, in part for this work.

Two honest limitations. The model was built for constitutional design in large polities, where members are assumed to act in self-interest and cannot easily leave — neither of which describes forty people who chose each other and can move out. And it treats the costs as though they were measurable, which in a community they are not; nobody can price an evening of goodwill. What transfers is the shape of the argument, not the arithmetic.

</Deep>
