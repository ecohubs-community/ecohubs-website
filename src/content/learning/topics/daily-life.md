<Quick>

Most weeks look ordinary. You go to work, cook some of your own meals, and close your door. What differs is the texture: two or three shared dinners you can skip, a few hours of work for the place, a couple of hours of meetings a month, and the constant low fact of being known.

The thing people underestimate is not the labour or the meetings. It is **privacy** — not the absence of a lock, but the absence of anonymity. Someone notices when you are low, when your car is gone, when you skip three dinners in a row.

For some people that is the entire point. For others it is unbearable, and they usually find out too late.

</Quick>

Descriptions of community life arrive in one of two registers: the brochure, where everyone is laughing over a long table, or the exposé, where it collapses into a governance dispute about the compost. Both are real days. Neither is a week.

## The week, roughly

**Meals.** Most established communities eat together two or three times a week, cooked on a rota in the <Gloss term="common-house">common house</Gloss>. Attendance is almost always optional, with a sign-up sheet so the cook knows numbers. Cook once a month for thirty people and you eat eleven meals you did not cook — the most-cited practical benefit of community life, and a real one.

**Work.** Two to six hours a week for the place: grounds, maintenance, cleaning shared rooms, admin, sitting on a circle. Communities vary enormously in whether this is counted and how strictly — see <Gloss term="work-quota">work quota</Gloss>.

**Meetings.** A whole-community meeting monthly or fortnightly, one to two hours, plus whatever working group you sit on. Two to five hours a month is typical in a settled community. In a forming one it can be triple that, and forming groups routinely underestimate it by a factor of two.

**Everything else.** Your job, your family, your own kitchen, your own evening. The mistake newcomers make is imagining community life *replaces* ordinary life. It sits on top of it.

The one that people who leave say they miss most is not on either side of that table. It is a default answer to the question *who would I call?*

## The work nobody put on the rota

Every community has a rota, and every community has a second layer of work that never reaches it: remembering that the insurance renews, noticing that a new member has not been included in anything for a fortnight, absorbing the awkwardness after a difficult meeting, being the person who always makes the tea.

<Gloss term="invisible-work">Invisible work</Gloss> is real, time-consuming, and distributes itself unevenly — very often along the same lines it does everywhere else. Communities that are honest about it name it and try to count it. Communities that are not describe it as somebody "just being good at that", which is how a person ends up doing it for eleven years.

## Noise, doors and privacy

Well-designed communities think in terms of a <Gloss term="privacy-gradient">privacy gradient</Gloss>: from fully public through semi-private to fully private, so a resident can regulate contact without having to announce that they want to be left alone.

Where it is missing — homes opening directly onto a shared kitchen, no defensible outdoor space, thin walls — people withdraw entirely instead, because the only available setting is *all* or *nothing*.

The privacy hardest to arrange is not spatial. It is not being observed. In a community of forty, people know roughly when you come home, whether you went to the meeting, and how you looked when you did. Nobody is spying; it is the arithmetic of living near people who pay attention.

<Callout type="caveat">

None of the good texture arrives automatically. A community with the architecture and none of the practice — a beautiful common house and no cooking rota — delivers almost none of it. What produces the week described here is habit, maintained deliberately, usually for years.

</Callout>

## Children and later life

The decades most accounts skip are the ones that decide it for a household.

Small children get adults who are not their parents and meals somebody else cooked; parents get opinions about their child from people they will see on Thursday. Teenagers are the hardest test of a community's culture, because being fifteen means becoming someone your parents did not design, and a small place where everyone knows you is a difficult setting for that.

At the other end, senior cohousing is designed around independent living with mutual support, and the literature is careful about the boundary: **co-care is a layer underneath formal care, not a replacement for it**. Communities that handle ageing well discuss it years before it arrives. Those that do not discover that willingness is not the same as capacity, usually at three in the morning.

<Deep title="Deep · a claim we went looking for and could not confirm">

The most-repeated benefit of raising children in community is that they can roam — that because neighbours know each other, children get a freedom their peers have lost.

A 2019 Canadian multi-site study of **1,699 children aged 8 to 12** measured close to exactly this: whether parents agreeing that "most adults who live in the neighborhood look out for other people's children" predicted children's independent mobility. **It did not** — the association was not significant in the final analysis. What was strongly associated, negatively, was parents' fear of traffic, crime and dangerous crossings.

That study is of ordinary Canadian neighbourhoods rather than intentional communities, and nobody appears to have run the equivalent inside one. But it points somewhere useful. If children do roam more in cohousing, the likeliest reason is not that everybody knows them — it is that the **cars are parked at the edge**, which is a design decision you can check on a site plan before you move in rather than a social quality you have to take on trust.

The general lesson is worth carrying into every claim in this topic: the pleasant explanation and the true one are not always the same, and site design is easier to verify than culture.

</Deep>
