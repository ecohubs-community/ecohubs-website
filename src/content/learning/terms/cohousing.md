Cohousing is an architecture before it is anything else: private, self-contained homes — each with its own kitchen and bathroom — clustered around shared facilities that the residents own and run together. The shared part almost always includes a **common house** with a large kitchen and dining room, and often a laundry, workshop, and guest rooms that individual homes are built small enough to do without.

Four features recur across almost every project that calls itself cohousing: residents participate in the design, the site is laid out to make encounter likely (cars parked at the edge, front doors facing a shared path), facilities are genuinely shared rather than merely communal-looking, and the community manages itself with no external landlord or provider.

## What it does not require

Shared income. Shared politics. Shared meals, even — most communities eat together two or three times a week, and attendance is optional. Cohousing is deliberately thin on ideology, which is exactly why it is the easiest form for people with conventional jobs and mortgages to join.

## An example

The form began in Denmark in the early 1970s — *bofællesskab*, "living community" — with Sættedammen, near Hillerød, generally cited as the first. It reached English-speaking countries largely through Kathryn McCamant and Charles Durrett, whose 1988 book gave the model its English name. Typical projects run to twenty or forty households, a size chosen deliberately: large enough that the common house is busy, small enough that everyone knows everyone.

<Callout type="caveat">
Cohousing is the most expensive form to start, because it means building or converting real housing to a shared design. It is also the easiest to join later, for the same reason — you buy or rent a home rather than being admitted to a shared economy.
</Callout>

## What it is often confused with

An <Gloss term="ecovillage" />, most of all. The two are not alternatives on the same axis: cohousing describes the *architecture and ownership*, an ecovillage describes the *purpose*. Plenty of ecovillages are built as cohousing, and plenty of cohousing has no ecological ambition at all. Confusing them is common enough that we wrote a page on it.
