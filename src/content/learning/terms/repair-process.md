A repair process is the route a community has agreed a conflict will take. Not a method — mediation, restorative circles, nonviolent communication are all methods — but the surrounding structure that decides how a dispute enters the system and how it leaves.

The method matters far less than four structural questions.

## The four questions

**Who may start it?** The most important one. If only the board, the founders or a majority can trigger the process, it is unavailable to precisely the person most likely to need it: someone new, alone, and in conflict with somebody established.

**What happens first?** Almost every workable system starts with the two people talking, usually with support. The purpose is not politeness; it is to stop the group being recruited into the dispute before either party has stated it plainly.

**Who holds it, and are they neutral?** In a community of thirty, the obvious mediator is often the person everybody trusts — who is also somebody's close friend and probably has a view. Small communities routinely need someone from outside.

**What can it conclude?** A process that can only recommend is a conversation. Repair needs the possibility of an outcome: an agreement, a rule change, a consequence, an apology on the record.

## How to test one from outside

Ask when it was last used, by whom, and what changed as a result. A process that has never been triggered in a ten-year-old community of forty is not evidence of harmony; it is evidence the process is unusable.

<Callout type="caveat">

No process resolves a conflict where one party has substantially more power than the other and the community will not say so. Mediation between unequals produces agreement rather than resolution — and the community usually misdiagnoses this as a failure of the method and goes looking for a better one.

</Callout>
