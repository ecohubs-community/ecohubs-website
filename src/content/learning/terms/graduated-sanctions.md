Graduated sanctions means a community's response to a broken agreement scales with how serious and how repeated the breach is. A first lapse gets a conversation; a persistent pattern gets something with more weight behind it.

The idea comes from Elinor Ostrom's study of common-pool resources — irrigation systems, fisheries, forests, some managed successfully for centuries — published as *Governing the Commons* in 1990, work that won the Nobel Memorial Prize in Economics in 2009. Graduated sanctions is one of eight design principles she found in the systems that lasted.

## Why communities need it

Most communities have the opposite: a rule with no stated consequence at all. That is a preference rather than a rule, and every member works out which is which inside a year.

What tends to fill the gap is worse than either extreme. With nothing written, a group either does nothing for years and then overreacts, or it applies whatever response the most annoyed person can push through. Both feel arbitrary to the person on the receiving end, and both teach everyone else that raising a breach is not worth the trouble.

## What a scale looks like

A workable ladder is short and stated in advance: a private conversation, then a conversation with a third person present, then a written note on the record, then a defined consequence — a suspended right, a charge, a required process — and at the far end, the grounds on which a community may ask somebody to leave.

The point is not severity. It is **predictability**: everyone can see where they are on the ladder and what the next step would be.

<Callout type="caveat">

Sanctions only work where breaches are noticed, which is the design principle immediately before this one. A community with no monitoring does not have a lenient system; it has an unenforced one, where the only breaches that get addressed are the ones committed by unpopular people.

</Callout>
