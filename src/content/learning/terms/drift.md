Drift is what happens to a community when nothing goes wrong.

The <Gloss term="agreements">agreements</Gloss> stop being read. Then they stop being taught to new members. Then they describe a place that no longer exists. Shared meals go from three a week to one, to occasionally, to "we should do that again". The work rota keeps existing and stops being followed. Nobody decides any of this and no meeting is held about it.

One day the community is a pleasant street where people are polite to each other, and everyone is vaguely sad without being able to say what was lost.

## Why it is the hardest to catch

Every other failure pattern announces itself. A money dispute is loud, a founder problem produces a specific argument, an unresolved conflict has people not speaking. Drift produces no event at all — which means there is never a moment when raising it feels proportionate.

It is also nobody's fault, which sounds like a mercy and is actually the problem: with no one to hold responsible, there is no obvious person to act.

## What slows it

**A review date written into the agreements themselves.** The cheapest defence anybody has found. Not a promise to review — a date.

**An induction that includes reading the text.** New members are the first to stop seeing the agreements, and the last to know there were any.

**Counting something.** Meals served, meetings held, hours contributed. A community that tracks one number will notice it falling long before it notices the feeling changing.

**Asking leavers what changed.** People who left five years apart will describe two different places, and the difference is the drift.

<Callout type="caveat">

Not all drift is decline. Communities legitimately change what they want — a group of thirty-year-olds and the same group at sixty need different things. The question is whether the change was chosen. A community that decided to stop eating together twice a week has adapted; one that just stopped has drifted.

</Callout>
