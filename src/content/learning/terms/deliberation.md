Deliberation is the reading and discussion window between a proposal being published and voting opening. Nothing is counted during it. That is the entire point.

Most groups deliberate without naming it — the conversation over dinner, the thread that runs for a week, the meeting where everyone talks it through. Naming it as a phase does two things that the informal version cannot. It tells everyone that the discussion is happening _now_, so people who were not at dinner are not quietly excluded. And it stops the group from voting on something in the same hour it first heard about it, which is where most regretted decisions come from.

## Why it has to be separate from voting

When discussion and voting run together, the first few votes become part of the discussion. People who have not made up their mind can see where the group is going, and a good number of them follow it rather than think about it. This is well documented in group decision research as a cascade, and it produces results that look like agreement and are closer to momentum.

Separating the phases is the cheapest available defence. It also gives an objection somewhere to go _before_ it becomes a block: a concern raised during deliberation can change the wording of the <Gloss term="motion">motion</Gloss>, while the same concern raised during voting can only stop it.

## How long

There is no correct number, and length should track stakes rather than convention. In practice communities settle on something like a day for operational matters, a week for anything that commits money or changes a rule, and considerably longer for changes to the community's own purpose or governance — the pattern behind <Gloss term="decision-tiers">decision tiers</Gloss>.

<Callout type="caveat">

A deliberation window is a container, not a conversation. Publishing a proposal for a week and having nobody read it produces the same decision as no window at all, with more paperwork. What makes it work is somebody whose job it is to notice silence and go and ask — usually the <Gloss term="facilitation">facilitator</Gloss>, and usually not the proposer.

</Callout>

The people most likely to miss the window are the ones with the least time: shift workers, parents of young children, anyone caring for someone. A group that consistently decides things in a week has, without meaning to, weighted its decisions toward whoever has evenings free.
