A veto window is a period after voting closes during which a member can still object, and a sustained objection stops the decision. Unlike a <Gloss term="cooling-off-period">cooling-off period</Gloss>, which expires on its own and gives nobody any new authority, a veto window deliberately hands something out: the ability to stop a result that has already passed.

That is a real power, and it should be granted deliberately or not at all.

## What it is for

The case for it is the member who was not there. Someone on a night shift, someone travelling, someone who has been in hospital — people whose absence is not indifference. A decision that passed while the four people it most affects were unreachable is procedurally sound and practically fragile, and a group that pushes ahead anyway usually pays for it later in a way that does not look like a governance problem at the time.

It is also the natural partner of a long <Gloss term="deliberation">deliberation</Gloss> period on the heaviest decisions. Where a community uses <Gloss term="consensus">consensus</Gloss> for changes to its own purpose or agreements, an objection window after the vote is what makes "we heard everyone" more than a hope.

## The cost, said plainly

Every veto window is a standing invitation to reopen a settled question. Groups that apply one to everything discover that no decision ever feels final, that proposers start campaigning after the vote rather than before it, and that the member most willing to use the window acquires influence nobody voted to give them. It is the same failure as an undefined <Gloss term="blocking-concern">blocking concern</Gloss>, arriving a week later.

Three things keep it workable:

- **Grounds.** A late objection has to clear the same bar as any other — a named harm to the group or its aim, not a preference that arrived late.
- **A named consequence.** What happens next: does the proposal return rewritten, go to a circle, or simply fall? A window that only stops things builds a queue nobody owns.
- **A scope.** Which decisions get one. Applying it only to the heaviest tier is what stops it becoming a general veto.

<Callout type="caveat">

A veto window cannot compensate for people not being asked in the first place. If the same members keep needing it, the problem is when and how proposals are published, not what happens after they pass. The window is treating a symptom, and it is worth checking which one.

</Callout>
