Facilitation is holding a meeting's process — whose turn it is, which stage the proposal is at, when a round is finished — while staying out of its content. The facilitator does not advocate. Their job is that the method the group agreed on actually gets followed.

That sounds procedural and minor. In practice it is the variable that most reliably separates communities whose meetings work from communities whose meetings are dreaded, more than the choice between <Gloss term="consensus">consensus</Gloss> and <Gloss term="consent">consent</Gloss> does.

## What the role actually does

Keeps the stage explicit, so nobody argues the merits while the group is still clarifying what is being proposed. Runs rounds, so the quiet get the same air as the fluent. Names when a decision has been reached, which sounds trivial and is the thing groups most often fail to do — leaving meetings that end without anyone certain what was agreed. And notices when a proposal is not ready and should be sent back rather than ground through.

## An example

The single most useful convention is **rotating the role**, and it is worth adopting even when someone is obviously good at it. A permanent facilitator accumulates informal power that no agreement granted them: they decide what gets discussed and when a thing is settled, and that is authority regardless of what the rules say. Rotation also spreads the skill, which is what a community actually needs when the one competent facilitator is away for a month.

The corollary is that a facilitator who has a strong view on the item should hand the role over for that item and say why. Groups that treat this as normal have fewer arguments about neutrality than groups that treat it as an accusation.

<Callout type="caveat">

Facilitation is a skill, not a personality. Communities often hand it to whoever is most confident in a room, which selects for exactly the wrong thing — the role rewards patience and attention to who has not spoken, not fluency.

</Callout>
