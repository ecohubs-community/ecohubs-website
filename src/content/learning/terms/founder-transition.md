Founder transition is the conversion of the informal authority that built a community into formal, transferable roles. It is one of the two or three things that most reliably decides whether a community outlives the people who started it.

The founders carried the project, made the early decisions, and were right to. Nothing about that is a criticism. The failure is specific: that authority is never written down, so it can neither be handed over nor argued with.

## What it looks like when it has not happened

Twelve years in, the founders still make most significant decisions and nothing about their role is recorded. This is easy to mistake for continuity and it has three consequences:

- **The group cannot disagree.** There is no defined authority to appeal, so disagreement becomes personal.
- **The group cannot continue.** Nothing was transferred, so when a founder wants to leave the community discovers it does not know how anything works.
- **The founders cannot put it down.** This is the part outsiders miss. Founders are frequently exhausted and cannot stop, because there is no mechanism by which stopping is possible.

## What a transition actually involves

Less drama than the phrase suggests, and more paperwork. Naming the roles the founders occupy in practice. Writing down what each may decide. Giving them terms. Documenting what only they know — the accounts, the suppliers, the history behind rules that look arbitrary. Then rotating something, publicly, while the founders are still there to be asked.

The best time to do it is years before anybody wants it, which is exactly why it is deferred.

<Callout type="caveat">

Watch for the transition that happens on paper only: roles are created, elections are held, and everyone still checks with the founder informally before deciding anything. That is not a transition, and it is harder to fix afterwards than doing nothing would have been — the community now believes the problem is solved.

</Callout>
