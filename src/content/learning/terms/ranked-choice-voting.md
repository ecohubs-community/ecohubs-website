Ranked-choice voting — also called instant-runoff — asks members to put the options in order of preference rather than picking one. If no option holds a majority of first preferences, the last-placed option is eliminated and each of its ballots transfers to that voter's next surviving choice. This repeats until one option is preferred by more than half of the remaining ballots.

It solves a specific and very ordinary problem: choosing among three or more options when picking one each would elect something most of the group did not want.

## The problem it fixes

Five candidates stand for facilitator. Under a single choice, someone can win with twenty-two per cent while seventy-eight per cent of the community preferred almost anyone else. The same thing happens with proposals — three variants of a plan split the support of the people who wanted change, and the option to do nothing wins on a minority.

Ranking removes the incentive to vote tactically. Nobody has to work out which candidate is "electable" and abandon the one they actually want, because supporting a long shot first costs nothing: if it is eliminated, the ballot moves to the next preference. In a community of forty, that guessing game is not hypothetical — it is exactly how a well-liked person ends up not standing.

## What it does not do

It elects one option. It does not carry a <Gloss term="motion">motion</Gloss>, and there is nothing for it to fail against — the result is a winner, not a yes or a no. A group that wants "adopt this or don't" needs a threshold vote, <Gloss term="consensus">consensus</Gloss> or <Gloss term="consent">consent</Gloss> instead.

It also produces a majority of the ballots still in play, not of everyone. If members rank only their first two choices, their ballots exhaust and stop counting, and the eventual winner can hold a majority of a shrunken pool. Worth knowing before quoting the number.

<Callout type="caveat">

Ranked-choice reliably beats a single choice for multi-option decisions, and it is not neutral or perfect — no ranking method can be, which is what Arrow's theorem establishes. It can, in known edge cases, eliminate an option that would have beaten every other one head to head. For a community choosing a facilitator or a build date, this is a curiosity. It is worth not overselling.

</Callout>

Its practical cost is explanation. A group has to be willing to explain elimination rounds once, and to publish them, because a result nobody can follow is a result nobody trusts.
