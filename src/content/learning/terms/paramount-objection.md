Under <Gloss term="consent">consent</Gloss>, a proposal passes unless someone raises a paramount objection. The word carries the whole method, and groups that adopt consent without defining it end up running unanimity under a new name.

An objection is paramount when it names a way the proposal would harm the group or stop it meeting its aim. It is not paramount when it expresses a preference, however well-informed. "I would have chosen a different builder" is a preference. "This spends the reserve we agreed in March to hold for winter repairs" is an objection: it names a specific harm, ties it to something the group already decided, and anyone can go and check.

## The three tests

- **Reasoned.** It comes with a reason, stated out loud. "I just don't like it" is a feeling, and a feeling worth hearing — but it belongs in <Gloss term="deliberation">deliberation</Gloss>, not at the close.
- **About the aim, not the taste.** The harm has to be to the community or its purpose. Being personally unenthusiastic is what <Gloss term="stand-aside">standing aside</Gloss> is for.
- **Checkable.** Someone else has to be able to evaluate it. An objection nobody can assess cannot be resolved, only outlasted.

## Who decides whether it stands

This is the part groups most often leave unwritten, and it is where the method actually lives. Someone has to determine whether a stated objection clears the bar — usually the <Gloss term="facilitation">facilitator</Gloss>, sometimes the circle whose <Gloss term="domain">domain</Gloss> it is, occasionally the group as a whole. Each choice has a cost. A facilitator deciding is fast and concentrates real power in a role people think of as administrative. The group deciding is slower and can turn into a vote about whether to allow a vote.

What is not workable is leaving it undefined, because then it is decided by whoever is most insistent.

<Callout type="caveat">

Upholding an objection is a judgement, not a calculation. No definition removes that, and no software makes it fair — a tool can record who decided and on what basis, which is more than most groups have, and it cannot tell you whether the call was right. That is why rotating the role matters more here than almost anywhere else.

</Callout>

An objection that stands should send the proposal back rather than kill it. Under consent the expected next move is a rewrite that addresses the named harm — which is why the record says _blocked_, not _failed_.
