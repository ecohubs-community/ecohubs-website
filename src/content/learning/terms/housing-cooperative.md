In a housing co-operative, the members do not individually own their homes. They collectively own the organisation that owns the building, and each member holds a share plus a right to occupy a particular home. You buy into the co-op rather than buying a flat.

This is a legal shell rather than a social form. A co-op can be a close intentional community or an ordinary block of flats whose residents happen to share a freehold — the structure says how ownership and decisions work, not how much anyone shares.

## What the structure changes

**Decisions about the building are collective by construction.** Repairs, rents, admissions and rules are decided by the membership, usually one vote per member rather than by size of holding.

**What you can sell varies enormously**, and this is the question to ask first. In a *market-rate* co-op, shares can be sold at whatever they will fetch, and the co-op behaves much like ordinary ownership. In a **limited-equity** co-op, the resale price is capped by a formula, which keeps homes affordable for the next member and means the current one does not capture the appreciation.

**Mortgages work differently.** Lending against a co-op share is a specialised product in most countries, and it is the practical obstacle that surprises people who assumed the finance would resemble a normal purchase.

## An example

The limited-equity model is the one to understand, because it makes the trade-off explicit: members give up the prospect of a windfall in exchange for a home that costs less to enter and stays affordable after they leave. Whether that is a sacrifice or the entire point depends on what the co-op was formed to do — and a co-op that has never written down which of the two it is will eventually have a painful meeting about it.

<Callout type="caveat">

"Co-operative" is a legal designation with specific meanings that differ by country. Before comparing two projects, check what the word means in each jurisdiction rather than assuming the arrangements match.

</Callout>
