Informal power is the influence people hold in a group without anyone having voted for it, written it down, or being able to take it back. It is not a failure of a community; it is what happens automatically, and the only question is whether it is visible.

Jo Freeman's essay *The Tyranny of Structurelessness* (1970, published 1972) made the argument that has never stopped being relevant here: **a structureless group is impossible**. Where structure is informal it forms around friendship networks and access to information, and produces an elite that is unaccountable precisely because the group denies it exists.

## Where it sits in a community

- The **founders**, whose objection carries weight nobody wrote down.
- The **long-timers**, who can end a discussion by remembering why a rule exists.
- Whoever **holds the information** — the accounts, the keys, the passwords, how the heating works.
- Whoever has the **most time**, which usually means retired, or without children.

None of these people is doing anything wrong. Informal power accumulates around competence and availability, and the people holding it are frequently the ones working hardest.

## What reduces it

Freeman's remedies transfer almost unchanged: delegate specific authority to specific people for specific tasks by an agreed procedure; require them to be accountable; **rotate the tasks** so nobody comes to own a role; allocate by ability and interest rather than by who volunteered first; and diffuse information as widely as possible.

That last one is the cheapest and least done. A community where two people can read the accounts has two people in charge, whatever its constitution says.

<Callout type="caveat">

"We don't really need a structure, we all get on" is the least reassuring sentence a community can say to a visitor. Formal structure is not the opposite of an egalitarian community — it is the mechanism by which one stays egalitarian.

</Callout>
