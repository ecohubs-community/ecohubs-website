A supermajority is any pass threshold above half. In community practice it is almost always two-thirds, occasionally three-quarters, and it is reached for when a bare majority feels like too thin a basis for what is being decided.

The instinct is sound. Twenty-one against twenty is a decision under majority rule and it is not a settled question, and communities that carry contentious changes on margins like that tend to relitigate them for years. Raising the bar means that what passes has real weight behind it.

## What it costs, exactly

Raising the threshold does not only make bad decisions harder to pass. It makes _all_ decisions harder to pass, and it hands a determined third of the group a standing veto over change.

That is not a criticism, it is the mechanism. A two-thirds rule is a deliberate transfer of power from the majority to any sufficiently large minority, and whether that is protection or paralysis depends entirely on what is being protected. Applied to the community's founding purpose, it is exactly right — a purpose that thirty-five per cent of members actively oppose is not a shared purpose. Applied to the annual budget, it means a third of the group can starve the community by declining to agree to anything.

This is the trade-off Buchanan and Tullock formalised in _The Calculus of Consent_ (1962): as the required share rises, the cost of reaching a decision rises and the cost of being overruled falls. There is no rule that is best for everything, only a rule that is best for a given class of decision — which is the argument for <Gloss term="decision-tiers">decision tiers</Gloss> rather than a single number.

## The details that decide it

Two-thirds of what? Of everyone eligible, or of those who voted, or of those who voted either way with abstentions set aside? These produce genuinely different outcomes, and a group that has not written down which one it means will discover the ambiguity on the day it matters most.

A threshold also does nothing on its own without a <Gloss term="quorum">quorum</Gloss>. Two-thirds of six people is four.

<Callout type="caveat">

A high threshold protects the status quo, not the vulnerable. Those coincide when the existing arrangement is fair and diverge sharply when it is not — a supermajority rule makes an unjust rule as hard to remove as a just one is to impose. Groups adopting one to protect a minority should check which minority the current arrangement already favours.

</Callout>
