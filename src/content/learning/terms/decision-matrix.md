A decision matrix is one table listing the kinds of decision a community makes, and for each one: who may make it, by what method, and inside what limits. It is the least glamorous artifact in community governance and the one whose absence causes the most trouble.

Everything else in a group's <Gloss term="agreements">agreements</Gloss> describes principles. This describes practice. It is the document a new member can read in five minutes and know how the place actually works.

## What a row looks like

| Decision            | Who decides        | Method              | Limit                         |
| ------------------- | ------------------ | ------------------- | ----------------------------- |
| Repairs under €500  | Maintenance circle | Circle consent      | Within annual budget          |
| Annual budget       | All members        | Two-thirds approval | —                             |
| Admitting a member  | All members        | Consensus           | After the full visitor period |
| Changing this table | All members        | Consensus           | Two-week objection window     |

The last row is the one people forget, and it is the most important. A matrix that cannot say how it is itself amended is a document that will be changed informally, by whoever notices first.

## Why it belongs in one place

Communities usually have this information. It is scattered across four years of minutes, a handbook nobody updated, and the memory of two long-standing members — which means that in practice the answer to "who decides this?" is "ask Marta". That is not a governance system. It is a dependency on a person, and it becomes visible at exactly the moment that person wants to leave.

Writing it as one table also exposes the gaps. Almost every group that builds one discovers two or three categories of decision that nobody had ever assigned, and at least one that two different bodies both believed was theirs.

The RCOS Standard makes this a required artifact of Layer 2, alongside an authority registry naming who currently holds each role. The pairing is deliberate: the matrix says what the roles may do, and the registry says who is in them today.

<Callout type="caveat">

A matrix records the authority a group has granted. It does not describe the authority people actually exercise — founders, long-timers and whoever holds the passwords will keep deciding things the table does not mention. Making the formal structure explicit is what allows the informal one to be named and argued with; it does not remove it.

</Callout>
