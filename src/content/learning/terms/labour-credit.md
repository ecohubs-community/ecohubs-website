A labour credit is one hour of work, recorded against the total a member is expected to contribute. Systems differ in the detail, but the principle is constant: **an hour is an hour**, whether it was spent running a business that earns the community money or cooking dinner for thirty people.

That equivalence is the whole point, and it is a deliberate rejection of how the surrounding economy values work.

## Why a community would bother

Without some accounting, the work that gets noticed is the work that produces money or takes place in public. Childcare, cleaning, remembering that the insurance renews, and sitting with someone who is struggling all disappear — not because anyone decides they do not matter, but because nothing counts them.

A credit system makes them countable. In practice this tends to have two effects a group can feel within months: the people doing invisible work stop feeling invisible, and the group discovers how much of it there actually is.

## What it costs

Bureaucracy, and the risk of turning a household into a timesheet. Members log hours; somebody administers the log; disputes arise about what qualifies. A community of ten does not need this and will resent it. A community of ninety with a <Gloss term="common-purse">common purse</Gloss> has few alternatives.

Most communities land somewhere in between, with a stated <Gloss term="work-quota">work quota</Gloss> and no formal credit accounting — trusting members to meet it, and dealing with shortfalls as a conversation rather than a balance.

<Callout type="caveat">

Watch what a system does _not_ count, because that is where the unfairness accumulates. Emotional labour and organisational memory are the two most commonly uncounted, and they distribute themselves along much the same lines they do everywhere else.

</Callout>
