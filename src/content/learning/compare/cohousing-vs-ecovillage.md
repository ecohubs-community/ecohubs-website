<Quick>

Cohousing describes **how the housing is arranged**: private self-contained homes clustered around shared facilities, run by the residents. Ecovillage describes **what the place is for**: a community consciously designing its systems to regenerate its environment. A project can be both. Most cohousing is not an ecovillage, and plenty of ecovillages look nothing like cohousing.

</Quick>

People use these two words as if they were points on the same scale — cohousing for the mild version, ecovillage for the committed one. They are not on the same scale at all. One answers *what does the built environment look like?* and the other answers *what is this place trying to do?*

## Cohousing is a shape

<Gloss term="cohousing">Cohousing</Gloss> came out of Denmark in the late 1960s — *bofællesskab*, "living community" — as an answer to a domestic problem rather than an ecological one: raising children and running a household in a nuclear family is isolating and inefficient. The design that emerged is remarkably consistent wherever it has spread. Private homes, deliberately modest. A common house with a kitchen big enough to cook for everyone. Cars pushed to the edge so the paths between homes are walkable and children can be let out of the door.

What makes it cohousing is that the residents run it. Not a landlord, not a developer who stayed on, not a charismatic founder. That is also what makes it slow: a cohousing group typically spends years in meetings before anything is built.

Crucially, **households keep their own money.** People pay a share of the common costs and otherwise remain financially independent. That single fact removes most of the disputes that break other kinds of community — and removes most of the interdependence that makes them meaningful to the people who want that.

## Ecovillage is an aim

<Gloss term="ecovillage">Ecovillage</Gloss> is a claim about purpose. The usual reference point is the Global Ecovillage Network's framing: a community consciously designing its social, ecological, economic and cultural systems to regenerate its environment. Note how much that leaves open. It says nothing about whether you own your home, whether there is a common kitchen, or how many people live there.

That breadth is why the word covers such different places. A three-household land project in Portugal and a settlement of several hundred both use it, honestly. It is also why the label carries less information than people expect when they search for it — knowing a place calls itself an ecovillage tells you what it is aiming at, not what living there is like.

<Callout type="caveat">

Neither term is certified or protected. Nobody audits a project before it calls itself an ecovillage, and "eco" appears in a good deal of ordinary property marketing. Treat both words as a starting question rather than an answer, and ask what is actually shared.

</Callout>

## When each is the right answer

**Cohousing suits you if** you want your own front door and your own finances, you want neighbours you actually know, and the ecological dimension is welcome but not the point. It is the lowest-risk form here: if it does not work out, you sell a home much like any other.

**An ecovillage suits you if** the ecological work *is* the point — the land, the water, the food, the long horizon — and you are willing to accept far more variation in what you are joining, including possibly shared labour, shared income, or a legal structure where you never personally own the land.

The honest middle is that many projects are both, and the useful question is never which label a place uses. It is: what do we actually share, who decides, and what happens if I leave? Those three answers predict daily life. The label predicts almost nothing.

<Deep title="Deep · where the confusion comes from">

Part of it is that the two movements grew up in different decades with different problems. Cohousing is a 1960s answer to domestic isolation, formalised in Denmark and exported. The ecovillage framing is largely a 1990s response to ecological crisis, and arrived with a network attached.

The rest is that an ecovillage often *adopts* cohousing architecture, because clustered private homes around shared facilities is a well-tested pattern that works. So the most visible ecovillages frequently look like cohousing, which is exactly the observation that makes people assume the words are interchangeable.

</Deep>
