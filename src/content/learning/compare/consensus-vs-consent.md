<Quick>

**Consensus** asks *do you agree?* — a proposal passes when everyone actively supports it. **Consent** asks *do you object, and why?* — a proposal passes when nobody can name a reasoned way it would harm the group or stop it meeting its aim. Consensus seeks enthusiasm; consent seeks tolerability. The practical difference is that consent puts the burden on the objector to say what is wrong, and expects the decision to be revisited.

</Quick>

The two words are used interchangeably in almost every community that uses either, and the swap is expensive. Groups adopt "consensus" from a handbook, run something closer to consent in practice, and then spend years arguing about a rule nobody has written down.

## What consent actually changes

<Gloss term="consent">Consent</Gloss> narrows the question. Not *do you like this?* but *can you live with it, and can you point to a way it would harm us?* A proposal passes when nobody can.

That narrowing does two things. It moves the bar from enthusiasm to tolerability, which is a much lower bar — and deliberately so. And it puts the work on the person objecting: an objection has to be reasoned and has to be about the group's aim rather than about taste. "I would have picked a different colour" is a preference. "This spends the repair fund we agreed to hold for winter" is an objection.

The phrase that carries the rest of it is **good enough for now, safe enough to try**. Consent expects to be revisited. A decision made this way is provisional by design, and that is precisely what makes it possible to say yes to something you are not thrilled about.

## What consensus is actually for

None of that makes <Gloss term="consensus">consensus</Gloss> the worse method. It is doing a different job, and it is better at that job.

Consensus produces buy-in that consent does not. When a group has genuinely talked a proposal into a shape everyone supports, the implementation is different — people carry it out because they own it rather than because it passed. In small groups doing emotionally loaded work, that is worth the extra hours.

It is also a better teacher. The amendment loop — raise a concern, change the proposal, test again — forces a group to hear each other in a way that a well-run consent round can skip. Groups that start with consensus and move to consent later often keep the listening habits, and they are the reason the second method works at all.

<Callout type="caveat">

Neither method fixes a group that cannot talk to each other. Both change how a decision is *closed*, not whether the underlying disagreement was heard. A community with an unresolved power problem will produce bad decisions under either.

</Callout>

## Where each breaks

Consensus fails to fatigue and to scale. Past ten or fifteen people, requiring universal agreement quietly hands a veto to whoever is most stubborn, most tired, or most articulate at eleven at night. The failure is rarely dramatic: attendance drops, decisions drift to whoever stayed, and proposals stop being tabled because nobody wants the meeting.

Consent fails to vagueness. The three common versions, in rough order of frequency: the group adopts the language without defining who may object and on what grounds, so everything is still unanimity wearing a new name; objections get raised but never resolved, so proposals stall in a queue nobody owns; or the review date is never set, and "good enough for now" silently becomes permanent.

## When each is the right answer

**Consensus suits you if** the group is small, the work is relational, and you would rather spend three hours reaching real agreement than twenty minutes reaching a decision. It suits founding groups particularly well, where what you are really building is the ability to disagree safely.

**Consent suits you if** the group is past a dozen people, decisions are frequent and mostly operational, or your meetings have started to feel like endurance. It is also the right answer if you have noticed that one person's reluctance can stop anything — consent gives you a principled way to distinguish that from a real objection.

Most communities that last end up using both: consent for the ordinary run of decisions, and something much closer to consensus for the small number that change what the community *is*. Writing down which decisions are which is the single most useful governance hour you will spend.

<Deep title="Deep · defining a blocking concern">

Whichever method you choose, the thing to write down is the **blocking concern**: who may raise one, and on what grounds. A group that has never defined this has not adopted a method — it has adopted a veto and hoped nobody uses it.

Most workable definitions require the objection to be about the group's stated aim rather than about preference, to be articulable ("here is the harm"), and to come with a willingness to help find an alternative. Many also specify what happens when an objection cannot be resolved: it goes to a named circle, or a review date is set, or the proposal is trialled with an agreed end.

The corresponding move under consensus is defining **standing aside** — disagreeing without stopping the group. Communities that use consensus successfully at any scale almost always have an explicit stand-aside convention, and the ones that do not are usually the ones describing their meetings as exhausting.

</Deep>
