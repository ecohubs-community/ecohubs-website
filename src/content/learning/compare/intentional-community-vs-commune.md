<Quick>

**Intentional community** is the umbrella term: a group who chose each other, live together or near each other, and have written down how they decide things. **Commune** is one option inside that umbrella — the one that pools income. Every commune is an intentional community; most intentional communities are not communes, and the majority have entirely private finances.

</Quick>

This is the most common confusion newcomers arrive with, and it is not really a confusion between two things. It is a category being mistaken for its most famous member — like using "terrier" to mean "dog".

## What the umbrella actually requires

An <Gloss term="intentional-community" /> is defined by three conditions, and the third does the sorting: people chose each other, they live together or nearby, and they have written down how decisions get made.

Chosen-ness rules out a street you happened to move into. Proximity rules out a network that only meets online. Written agreements rule out a group of friends sharing a house on the assumption that goodwill will hold — and it is the absence of that third condition, far more than any failure of idealism, that ends most attempts.

Notice what is *not* in the definition: no ecological commitment, no shared income, no shared politics, no communal meals. A housing co-op in a city and a land project in the hills can both be intentional communities and share nothing else.

## Why "commune" swallowed the category

The word carries a picture — American and West German experiments of the late 1960s, most of which did not last. That picture is vivid, widely reproduced, and about fifty years old, and it is what most people are imagining when they hear any of these words.

The result is a persistent mismatch. Someone reads about <Gloss term="cohousing" /> in the paper, pictures a commune, and either dismisses it as impractical or arrives with expectations of a shared economy that does not exist. Community groups spend a surprising share of their public communication correcting this.

<Callout type="caveat">

The reverse error is worth watching too. Because "commune" sounds dated, some communities that genuinely do pool income avoid the word and describe themselves only as an intentional community. That is accurate, but it makes the most consequential fact about joining them harder to find. Ask directly.

</Callout>

## When each word is the right one

**Use "intentional community"** when you mean the category, or when you do not yet know what a particular place shares. It is the honest word for a search, a directory or a first conversation, precisely because it commits to so little.

**Use "commune"** only when income is genuinely pooled. It is a specific, checkable claim, and using it loosely is what created the confusion in the first place.

If you are trying to work out what a place is actually like, neither word will tell you. Three questions will: **what do we hold in common — land, money, decisions, or only goodwill? Who decides, and how? And what happens if I leave?** Every form on this page answers those differently, and the answers predict daily life far better than any label.

<Deep title="Deep · what the umbrella covers">

Working outward from the least to the most shared:

**Housing co-operatives** share ownership of the building and nothing else. Members hold shares rather than deeds, decisions about the property are collective, and finances are otherwise private.

**Cohousing** shares buildings, land and the management of them, with private self-contained homes and separate household finances.

**Ecovillages** share a purpose — regenerating the place they sit in — and vary enormously in everything else. Many are built as cohousing.

**Community land trusts** separate ownership of land from ownership of buildings, holding the land in trust permanently to keep housing affordable. This is a legal shell rather than a social form, and it sits underneath several of the others.

**Communes** share income, and usually property.

A single project frequently occupies more than one row: an ecovillage laid out as cohousing on land held by a community land trust is an ordinary combination, not an exotic one. Which is another way of saying the labels describe overlapping dimensions rather than a ladder of commitment.

</Deep>
