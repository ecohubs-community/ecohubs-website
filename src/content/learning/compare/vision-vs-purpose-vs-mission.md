<Quick>

**Purpose** is why the community exists. It should outlive every project, and changing it should be the hardest thing the community can do.

**Vision** is the picture of the place you are trying to become. It is motivating, it is not a rule, and nobody can be in breach of it.

**Mission** is what you are doing about it now. It has a date, it gets revised, and finishing it is a success rather than an identity crisis.

Most founding documents fuse all three into one paragraph adopted once at the start. That is why so many communities cannot change a plan without it feeling like a betrayal.

</Quick>

Almost every community has a founding statement, and almost none can tell you which of these three it is.

That sounds like a semantic complaint. It is not. These three things have completely different **shelf lives** and should be changeable by completely different means — and when they live in one paragraph, the whole paragraph inherits the strictest rule in it. A community that wants to stop running the café now has to reopen the sentence about why it exists, in the same meeting, with the founders in the room.

## Purpose is the one that is constitutional

A purpose states the enduring reason the community exists. The sharpest test is that **it cannot be completed**. "To steward this land as a working farm held in common" cannot be ticked off. "To convert the barn into six dwellings" can — which makes it a project wearing a purpose's clothes, and communities that adopt one of those spend the years after completion in a strange, unnameable drift, having quietly achieved the only thing they ever wrote down.

The second test is that a decision can genuinely contradict it. That is what makes a purpose load-bearing rather than decorative: if no allocation of money, land or labour could ever be inconsistent with your purpose, it is not doing constitutional work — it is a mood.

Which is why a purpose belongs at the top <Gloss term="decision-tiers">tier</Gloss>, alongside the invariants, and should be difficult to change. Not because founders' words are sacred. Because everybody who joined did so on the basis of it, and a purpose an ordinary majority can amend on a Tuesday is a promise the community has not actually made.

**One purpose, not several.** Communities routinely carry two — a place to live well together, and a demonstration project for the wider world — and never rank them. Both are honourable and they conflict constantly: about visitors, about money, about how much of the year is spent hosting. The failure is not having two. It is never having said which one yields.

## Vision is a picture, and nobody can breach it

A vision describes the place as you hope to find it in twenty years: the woodland grown in, the second generation staying, the workshop paying for itself. It is written to be read aloud, and its job is to make the effort legible to the people making it.

The mistake is treating it as an obligation. A vision that says "everyone contributes joyfully" is aspirational when it is on the wall and coercive the moment it is quoted at somebody in a meeting. Visions do not have breaches, because there is no way to comply with a picture. If your community can put somebody in the wrong by citing the vision statement, what you have written is an unenforceable identity constraint, and it will do the damage of a rule with none of the protections of one.

Keep it, though. Communities that discard the vision as fluff usually discover they have nothing to hand a prospective member that explains why any of this is worth it.

## Mission is the one with a date

A mission is the current answer to what we are doing about it — and it is the only one of the three that is supposed to change. Three years of work, a handful of named outcomes, somebody accountable for each.

Two things follow from that. Missions should be **easy to change**, at the ordinary decision tier, because a plan you cannot revise without a constitutional process is a plan you will quietly abandon instead of amending. And missions should be **allowed to finish**. A community that completes its mission and celebrates it has done the thing; a community that treats "we did it" as an existential problem has been using the mission to carry the purpose all along.

<Callout type="caveat">

Nobody polices this vocabulary, and plenty of good communities use the words the other way round — "our mission" for the enduring reason and "our purpose" for the current plan. The Global Ecovillage Network, business consultancies and your neighbours will all define them differently.

So do not correct anybody's usage. What matters is not which label goes where; it is that **the three things exist separately, with different change rules**. A community whose founding document is called a mission statement but is treated as constitutional has got the substance right and the word wrong, which is much the better mistake.

</Callout>

## The test that separates them, in one question each

Take whatever your community has written down and ask:

- **Could we achieve this and be finished?** If yes, it is a mission, whatever it is called.
- **Could somebody be in the wrong for acting against it?** If no, it is a vision — so do not put it where rules live.
- **Would changing this make people wonder whether to stay?** If yes, it is your purpose, and it should be protected accordingly.

Most communities find, doing this, that they have one document that answers all three inconsistently, and no record of when any of it was last agreed.

## What goes wrong when they are one document

Three specific failures, and they are common enough to be predictable.

**The plan cannot change.** Everything is fused to the constitutional layer, so revising this year's priorities means reopening the founding text. The community avoids this, sensibly, and the plan simply stops being accurate instead.

**Or the purpose changes by accident.** The opposite failure and the more expensive one. The document gets edited to reflect current activity — reasonably, a bit at a time — and eighteen months later the stated reason for existing has moved without any decision that anyone would recognise as constitutional. This is [mission drift](/learn/failures/mission-drift-competing-purposes), and it is the least dramatic way a community becomes something the people who joined it did not join.

**And nobody can say which version is in force.** Where one document does three jobs, it is revised often enough to accumulate versions and treated as sacred enough that nobody dates them. The fix is clerical: an adoption record and a summary of what changed, on each — <Gloss term="ratification">ratification</Gloss> that leaves a trace.

The remedy for all three is the same, and it is a morning's work: separate the three into three documents, write down which tier may change each, and date them.

RCOS takes a firmer position than this page does: of the three, **only purpose exists in the specification at all.**

A community **must define exactly one primary purpose** (§2.1.1), and that purpose **"MUST describe the enduring reason for the community's existence and MUST NOT be a short-term goal, project, or strategy"** (§2.1.2) — the distinction on this page, written as a conformance requirement. It must be stable across time and changeable only through a constitutional decision (§2.1.3).

Secondary purposes **may** be defined, but must not conflict with or override the primary one (§2.1.4) — which is the "say which one yields" problem, resolved by requiring a rank rather than trusting a community to find one during an argument.

Then the clause that makes a purpose testable rather than decorative: **no action, decision, or allocation of resources may materially contradict the stated primary purpose** (§2.1.5). Under that rule a purpose nothing could ever contradict is not a purpose, and a budget is evidence about whether you have one.

Vision and mission are simply not constitutional objects here. That is not an oversight — Layer 0 governs what must not be violated, and neither of them is that kind of thing.

</Rcos>
