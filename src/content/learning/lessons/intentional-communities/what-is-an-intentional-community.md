<Quick>

Three things have to be true at once: people chose each other, they live together or close by, and they have written down how they decide. Miss any one and it is a neighbourhood, a houseshare, or a marketing brochure.

</Quick>

An <Gloss term="intentional-community" /> is not defined by solar panels, a shared kitchen, or a manifesto. It is defined by explicit agreement.

## The test that actually sorts them

People argue about labels because they are arguing about different things — architecture, ideology, legal form, size. Set those aside and ask what the group holds in common: a <Gloss term="cohousing">cohousing</Gloss> group shares facilities, an <Gloss term="ecovillage" /> shares an ecological design, a <Gloss term="commune" /> shares income.

<Callout type="caveat">
Definitions in this field are contested, and communities often describe themselves in more than one way. Where a place calls itself something we would classify differently, we use its own word.
</Callout>

<Deep title="Deep · where the definitions come from">

The Foundation for Intentional Community and the Global Ecovillage Network use overlapping but non-identical criteria, which is why published counts of "how many communities exist" vary by an order of magnitude.

</Deep>
